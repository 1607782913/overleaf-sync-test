\begin{abstract}
Contrastive learning (CL) learns transferable representations from large-scale unlabeled data, but its reliance on weakly curated pre-training datasets makes it vulnerable to data-poisoning backdoor attacks. We study poisoned-sample detection and cleansing for unlabeled CL datasets. Our framework incorporates a probe-transformed trusted reference set into unsupervised encoder training. After training, the same fixed, pre-trained semantic probe is applied to the training samples, and the normalized backbone features of the transformed training and reference samples are jointly embedded with UMAP. DBSCAN is then applied to the training embeddings, and samples outside the largest non-noise cluster are flagged as suspicious under a minority-poisoning assumption. Preliminary BYOL experiments show strong poisoned-sample separation for SSL-Backdoor and CTRL on CIFAR; however, the detector fails to recover CorruptEncoder poisons in the evaluated CIFAR settings, and two ImageNet-100 configurations remain incomplete. These results demonstrate the potential of semantic probing while identifying important limitations that must be resolved before claiming a general defense.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Contrastive learning, self-supervised learning, backdoor attacks, poisoned sample detection, data cleansing.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ontrastive} learning (CL) has become a widely adopted paradigm for learning transferable representations from large-scale unlabeled data. Representative CL methods learn consistent representations across different augmented views of the same sample~\cite{Chen2020SimCLR,He2020MoCo,Grill2020BYOL}. The resulting encoder can be transferred to a variety of downstream tasks with limited labeled data. However, CL commonly relies on large, weakly curated datasets collected from public sources, creating an opportunity for an adversary to inject poisoned samples during pre-training. A backdoored encoder can preserve clean-data utility while causing a downstream classifier to predict an attacker-chosen target class for trigger-embedded inputs~\cite{Carlini2022Poisoning,Saha2022SSLBackdoor,Liu2022PoisonedEncoder}. Unlike supervised backdoor attacks, CL attacks must establish the trigger--target association through augmentation-driven representation learning without direct label supervision. This difference changes the learning dynamics and feature distributions of poisoned samples, limiting the transferability of defenses designed for supervised learning~\cite{Li2023CTRL,Li2024Difficulty}.

These challenges motivate defenses that intervene before a compromised encoder is transferred to downstream tasks. We therefore study training-data cleansing: detecting and removing poisoned samples from an unlabeled pre-training dataset before training a final encoder on the cleaned data. This setting differs from model-level detection and sanitization, as pursued by DECREE and SSL-Cleanse~\cite{Feng2023DECREE,Zheng2024SSLCleanse}. Existing data-centric defenses provide several useful directions. PatchSearch detects patch-based poisons by localizing candidate triggers and training a poison classifier~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Confusion Training and ASSET actively increase the separability between clean and poisoned samples through additional optimization~\cite{Qi2023Proactive,Pan2023ASSET}. AUTO mines relatively clean and poison-concentrated subsets from the untrusted training data and iteratively refines them as supervisory signals~\cite{Chen2025AUTO}. Although these methods demonstrate the feasibility of poisoned-sample cleansing, they also reveal trade-offs among trigger generality, feature separability, optimization cost, and reliance on trusted data.

A central challenge is the limited separability between clean and poisoned samples in the feature space learned from the poisoned dataset. Poisoned samples retain their original image content, while the backdoor is implanted as an additional trigger--target association. Existing attacks establish this association using visible patches, frequency-domain perturbations, augmentation-aware poison construction, or bi-level trigger optimization~\cite{Saha2022SSLBackdoor,Li2023CTRL,Zhang2024CorruptEncoder,Sun2024BLTO}. Consequently, poisoned samples may not form a distinct cluster in the original feature space, and a detection criterion tailored to one trigger type may not generalize to another~\cite{Pan2023ASSET,Li2024Difficulty}. Patch localization is effective for compact spatial triggers but is less suitable for distributed or optimized perturbations~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Optimization-based detectors can improve feature separability, but often require repeated training and reliable clean or poison-concentrated supervision~\cite{Qi2023Proactive,Pan2023ASSET,Chen2025AUTO}. These limitations motivate an active transformation that amplifies the feature differences between clean and poisoned samples before clustering.

Our key hypothesis is that a semantic probe can expose feature differences caused by an implanted backdoor association. We use a fixed, pre-trained probe associated with a defender-selected target class, which may differ from or coincide with the attacker's target class. When the two target classes differ, the probe-induced feature alignment may conflict with the trigger--target association already encoded in poisoned samples, producing different feature distributions for clean and poisoned data. This competition is a testable mechanism hypothesis rather than a secrecy assumption. The current detector evaluates the separability of probe-transformed features and does not explicitly measure the displacement between original and transformed features.

The detection pipeline first applies the pre-trained probe generator to a trusted reference set and jointly trains an encoder on the potentially poisoned unlabeled dataset and the probe-transformed reference samples using the same unsupervised CL objective. After training, the same probe is applied to every training sample, and we extract and $L_2$-normalize the backbone features of the probe-transformed training and reference images.

The probe-transformed training and reference features are jointly mapped to two dimensions with UMAP using cosine distance~\cite{McInnes2018UMAP}. After standardizing the training coordinates, we apply DBSCAN to identify dense clusters and noise points~\cite{Ester1996DBSCAN}. Under the assumption that poisoned samples are a minority, the largest non-noise cluster is treated as clean, whereas samples in the remaining clusters and noise are flagged as suspicious. The reference features influence the shared UMAP embedding but are not included in DBSCAN and do not directly determine which cluster is retained. The implemented detector is therefore reference-assisted during manifold construction and uses a largest-cluster rule for poisoned-sample detection.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a reusable semantic probe for poisoned-sample detection to transform the feature distributions of clean and poisoned samples.
    \item We incorporate probe-transformed trusted reference samples into unsupervised encoder training and subsequent joint feature analysis without using their labels as supervision.
    \item We design a density-based detection rule that requires neither poison annotations nor a known poison ratio: the largest DBSCAN cluster is retained, while other clusters and noise points are flagged as suspicious.
    \item We identify the assumptions and failure modes of the current detector and define the end-to-end evaluations needed to assess post-cleansing attack success, clean accuracy, robustness, and computational cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}
\subsection{Backdoor Attacks on Contrastive Learning}
Data-poisoning backdoor attacks on CL inject a small set of crafted samples into an unlabeled pre-training dataset to backdoor the learned encoder. The attack objective is twofold: a downstream classifier built on the backdoored encoder should predict the attacker-chosen target class for trigger-embedded inputs while retaining normal accuracy on clean inputs. Because the encoder is reused across downstream tasks, the implanted trigger--target association can persist after linear evaluation or task-specific fine-tuning~\cite{Carlini2022Poisoning}. SSL-Backdoor stamps a fixed patch trigger on images from the target class and exploits augmentation-based representation learning to associate the trigger with that class~\cite{Saha2022SSLBackdoor}. PoisonedEncoder optimizes poison construction using reference inputs and supports multiple target inputs or classes without controlling the victim's pre-training procedure~\cite{Liu2022PoisonedEncoder}.

Subsequent attacks broaden both the trigger space and the poison-generation strategy. CTRL injects an augmentation-resistant frequency-domain trigger and exploits representation invariance to achieve high attack success at a low poison ratio~\cite{Li2023CTRL}. CorruptEncoder analyzes the role of random cropping and constructs each poison by combining a target-class reference object, a trigger, and a background image so that contrastive views strengthen the desired association~\cite{Zhang2024CorruptEncoder}. BLTO formulates trigger generation as a bi-level optimization problem: the inner loop simulates the victim CL procedure, whereas the outer loop optimizes a trigger generator to keep trigger-injected samples close to target-class samples in the embedding space~\cite{Sun2024BLTO}. These attacks differ in trigger visibility, spatial locality, and interaction with data augmentation, making detectors based on a single trigger signature inherently limited.

More fundamentally, clean representation learning and backdoor injection are coupled through the same CL objective. Prior analysis shows that this coupling can make poisoned samples difficult to separate from clean samples in the learned feature space~\cite{Li2024Difficulty}. Consequently, defenses based only on unmodified features, training losses, or class-conditional statistics may transfer poorly across CL algorithms and attack mechanisms. Our method instead applies a controlled semantic probe and performs detection on the resulting probe-transformed features.

\subsection{Backdoor Defenses for Contrastive Learning}
Existing defenses can be broadly categorized as model-centric or data-centric. Model-centric defenses inspect a pre-trained encoder for backdoor behavior or sanitize the encoder after training. DECREE searches for a small perturbation that causes diverse inputs to produce similar features and uses the recovered perturbation to detect backdoored encoders~\cite{Feng2023DECREE}. SSL-Cleanse combines feature clustering, trigger inversion, and backdoor unlearning to detect and mitigate Trojans in self-supervised encoders~\cite{Zheng2024SSLCleanse}. These methods are applicable when only the pre-trained encoder is available, but they do not directly produce a cleaned pre-training dataset.

Data-centric defenses detect and filter poisoned samples before training a final model on the cleaned dataset. PatchSearch first trains an SSL encoder on the poisoned data, localizes candidate trigger patches, measures their ability to alter cluster assignments when pasted onto other images, and trains a poison classifier from highly suspicious samples~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Its patch-localization mechanism is effective for compact spatial triggers but does not naturally extend to distributed frequency-domain or input-dependent triggers. Confusion Training uses deliberately incorrect labels to amplify the difference between clean and poisoned samples~\cite{Qi2023Proactive}. Although designed for supervised learning, it motivates active feature separation rather than passive outlier detection.

ASSET extends active separation to supervised learning, SSL, and transfer learning through a nested offset optimization between an untrusted training set and a small clean base set~\cite{Pan2023ASSET}. Its clean base set provides a reference for separating poisoned samples, but a distribution mismatch between the base and training data can dominate the backdoor-related difference. AUTO avoids external clean data by mining a poison-concentrated positive set and a mostly clean negative set from the untrusted dataset, then bootstrapping the two sets to refine the supervision signal~\cite{Chen2025AUTO}. Our framework uses trusted data differently: probe-transformed reference samples are combined with the untrusted dataset during encoder training under the same unsupervised CL objective. During detection, the same probe is applied to the training samples, and the transformed training and reference features are jointly embedded with UMAP. The reference samples do not enter DBSCAN, and their labels are not used as supervision. This design avoids an additional active-separation optimization stage, but requires direct evaluation of its sensitivity to the reference distribution and the largest-cluster assumption.

\subsection{Representation-Based Anomaly Detection}
Feature-based analysis is a major direction in poisoned-sample detection. Spectral Signatures centers deep features and removes samples with large projections onto a dominant singular direction associated with poisoning~\cite{Tran2018Spectral}. SPECTRE strengthens spectral detection through robust covariance estimation, which can expose a weak poison signal at low poison ratios~\cite{Hayase2021SPECTRE}. Beatrix characterizes high-order activation correlations with Gram matrices and learns class-conditional clean statistics, enabling the detection of input-dependent triggers~\cite{Ma2023Beatrix}. These methods show that backdoor poisoning can leave detectable structure in learned features, but their supervised formulations commonly rely on class labels, predicted classes, or coherent within-class feature distributions.

These conditions are not directly available for an unlabeled CL dataset. Dataset-wide statistics can be dominated by semantic diversity, whereas partitioning samples with a backdoored encoder can preserve the trigger--target association that the detector seeks to identify~\cite{Li2024Difficulty}. We therefore analyze normalized backbone features after applying the semantic probe. UMAP builds a shared neighborhood graph from the probe-transformed training and reference features~\cite{McInnes2018UMAP}, and DBSCAN clusters the standardized training embeddings without requiring the number of clusters in advance~\cite{Ester1996DBSCAN}. The final decision uses cluster density and size: it retains the largest non-noise cluster rather than selecting the cluster closest to a reference centroid.

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:threatmodel}
\subsection{Attacker's Goal and Capabilities}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{b}\cup\mathcal{D}_{p}$ denote the unlabeled pre-training dataset, where $\mathcal{D}_{b}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{p}$ are the benign (clean) and poisoned subsets, respectively. The poison ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \rho=\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{p}|}{|\mathcal{D}|}.
    \label{eq:poisoningratio}
\end{equation}
The defender does not know $\rho$; we consider the conventional data-poisoning setting in which poisoned samples are a minority. The attacker selects a target class $y_t$, a trigger parameter $\tau$, and a trigger transformation $\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\cdot)$. The trigger may be a visible patch, a distributed frequency-domain pattern, or an optimized input-dependent perturbation. Depending on the attack, poisoned samples may be generated from target-class images, reference objects, or attack-specific optimization, and are processed by the same pre-training pipeline as clean samples.

The attack has two objectives. First, a downstream classifier $g_{\phi}\circ f_{\theta}$ built on the backdoored encoder should predict $y_t$ for trigger-embedded inputs. Second, the encoder and downstream classifier should retain their clean-data utility. The attacker can inject or replace samples in $\mathcal{D}$ but cannot modify the defender's detection code, trusted reference set, or post-cleansing training procedure. We adopt a strong knowledge assumption: the attacker may know the CL algorithm, encoder architecture, augmentation pipeline, training hyperparameters, probe generator, probe target class, and complete cleansing algorithm, and may optimize poisons through a surrogate CL pipeline. The defense therefore does not rely on keeping the probe or its target class secret. We consider both $y_t\neq y_r$ and the adaptive same-target setting $y_t=y_r$, where $y_r$ denotes the defender-selected probe target class.

\subsection{Defender's Knowledge and Capabilities}
The defender has full access to the unlabeled pre-training dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and can train an encoder, extract backbone features, and apply input transformations. The defender does not know the class labels, poison annotations, trigger pattern, target class, attack type, or poison ratio. The detector must therefore operate without pre-specifying the number of poisoned samples or semantic clusters.

In addition, the defender holds a trusted reference set $\mathcal{R}$ containing 1,300 clean images from one manually selected class $y_r$. The reference images may come from a different dataset and need not match the class distribution or data-collection process of $\mathcal{D}_{b}$; they only need a compatible visual modality and input format. Their labels are used solely to select the probe target class and never enter a supervised loss, clustering decision, or poison supervision. Because the same number of reference images is used for datasets of different sizes, $|\mathcal{R}|$ is treated as a fixed resource budget rather than a constant fraction of $|\mathcal{D}|$.

Let $G$ denote the fixed semantic probe generator associated with class $y_r$. The generator is pre-trained once and reused across datasets and encoder settings. Before encoder training, the defender applies $G$ to every reference image and trains the encoder on $\mathcal{D}\cup G(\mathcal{R})$ using the same unsupervised CL objective and augmentation pipeline; the reference labels do not enter the loss. After training, $G$ is applied to every sample in $\mathcal{D}$, with each perturbation clipped to an $\ell_{\infty}$ radius of $8/255$. The backbone features of $G(\mathcal{D})$ and $G(\mathcal{R})$ are $L_2$-normalized and jointly embedded with UMAP. DBSCAN is fitted only to the standardized coordinates of the training samples. Ground-truth poison indices are used only to evaluate detection performance and never affect the clustering decision.

\subsection{Assumptions and Defense Objective}
We make two primary assumptions. First, $\mathcal{R}$ is clean and protected from poisoning or replacement. Second, after semantic probing, poisoned samples do not form the largest density-connected component, although their exact prevalence is unknown. The second assumption is stronger than simply requiring a low poison ratio: if clean samples split into several semantic clusters, the largest DBSCAN cluster may not cover the full clean distribution. We make no secrecy assumption about the probe generator, its target class, or the detection procedure. Instead, the relationship between the attack target $y_t$ and probe target $y_r$ is treated as an experimental factor; the same-target setting $y_t=y_r$ directly tests the method when the hypothesized competition between two target associations is absent.

Let $\delta(x_i)\in\{0,1\}$ be the cleansing decision for $x_i\in\mathcal{D}$, where $1$ denotes a suspicious sample. The estimated poisoned set and the cleaned dataset are
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{p}=\{x_i\in\mathcal{D}:\delta(x_i)=1\},\qquad
    \widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{b}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{p}.
    \label{eq:cleansingsets}
\end{equation}
The detection objective is to maximize recovery of $\mathcal{D}_{p}$ while minimizing the removal of clean samples. The end-to-end defense objective is to train a final encoder on $\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{b}$ that achieves a low attack success rate on trigger-embedded inputs while preserving clean accuracy. We therefore evaluate both sample-level detection metrics---including precision, recall, and false-positive rate---and post-cleansing model metrics, including attack success rate and clean accuracy. A clustering metric alone is insufficient to establish defense effectiveness.
\section{Motivation and Key Observations}
\label{sec:motivation}

This section investigates why poisoned samples are difficult to
identify from their original representations and how diagnostic
probing can expose their hidden backdoor behavior. We first analyze
the representation overlap caused by contrastive backdoor learning,
then show that a semantic probe can amplify the difference between
benign and poisoned samples. These observations motivate the
detection framework introduced in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.


\subsection{Limitations of Original-Feature Detection}
\label{sec:original_feature}

A straightforward cleansing strategy is to detect poisoned samples
directly from the feature space of a suspicious encoder. Such
approaches generally assume that poisoned samples form abnormal
clusters or feature outliers. However, this assumption is weak for
contrastive-learning backdoors.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:original_overlap}, poisoned samples do not
necessarily form an isolated cluster in the original representation
space. Instead, they substantially overlap with benign samples,
especially those associated with the attack target semantics.
Consequently, clustering or outlier detection directly on the
original features provides limited separability.

\subsection{Why Poisoned Samples Overlap with Target Semantics}
\label{sec:target_overlap}

This phenomenon originates from the learning mechanism of
contrastive backdoors. Contrastive learning encourages augmented
views to learn consistent representations, while a successful
backdoor additionally establishes an association between the
attacker's trigger and a target semantic concept. Consequently,
trigger-related representations are encouraged to approach the
target semantic region during poisoned pre-training.

Let $\mathbf{z}_p$ denote the representation of a poisoned sample
and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t$ denote the representation centroid of the
attack target class. A successful attack tends to increase

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{sim}
    (\mathbf{z}_p,\boldsymbol{\mu}_t),
\end{equation}

making poisoned samples increasingly similar to benign target-class
samples. Figure~\ref{fig:target_alignment} provides both a
representation-space visualization and quantitative similarity
analysis. This target-semantic alignment explains why poisoned
samples may be difficult to identify as conventional feature
outliers.

\subsection{Diagnostic Probing Amplifies Poison--Clean Differences}
\label{sec:probe_observation}

The above observation motivates us to replace passive feature
inspection with active diagnostic probing. We apply the same
semantic probe $G(\cdot)$ to every sample and compare the resulting
representations. For an input $x_i$, let

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i=f_{\theta}(x_i), \qquad
    \mathbf{z}_i^{p}=f_{\theta}(G(x_i)).
\end{equation}

Although benign and poisoned samples may overlap before probing,
we observe that they respond differently to the same semantic
intervention. Poisoned samples already contain an attacker-induced
trigger--target association, which can modulate their response to
the newly introduced probe. As a result, probing increases the
difference between the two populations.

Figure~\ref{fig:probe_response} illustrates this effect from three
perspectives: feature-space separability, representation change,
and local-neighborhood variation. In particular, the probe causes
different changes in representation magnitude and neighborhood
structure for benign and poisoned samples, making the originally
entangled populations more distinguishable.

\subsection{Design Implications}
\label{sec:design_implications}

The above observations provide two implications for our defense.
First, poisoned-sample detection should operate on probe-transformed
representations rather than relying solely on the original feature
space. Second, the discriminative information introduced by probing
is strongly reflected in local neighborhood structure.

Accordingly, our framework jointly analyzes the probe-transformed
training and trusted reference samples using UMAP to preserve local
neighborhood relationships. DBSCAN is then used to identify
density-separated suspicious samples without requiring the poison
ratio in advance. The detailed framework is presented in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.
