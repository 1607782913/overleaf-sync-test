\begin{abstract}
Contrastive learning has emerged as an effective paradigm for learning transferable representations from large-scale unlabeled data. However, its reliance on uncurated pre-training data exposes it to data-poisoning backdoor attacks. In this work, we study backdoored sample cleansing for unlabeled contrastive-learning datasets and develop an active diagnostic probing framework. The key idea is to transform difficult-to-separate static representations into probe-induced response representations, calibrate benign responses using a small cross-domain reference set, preserve local response topology with UMAP, and identify suspicious samples with reference-anchored DBSCAN. Extensive experiments will evaluate cleansing effectiveness, post-cleansing security and utility, and computational overhead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ontrastive} learning (CL) has become a fundamental paradigm for self-supervised representation learning, enabling neural networks to learn transferable visual representations from large-scale unlabeled datasets. By encouraging different augmented views of the same instance to produce similar representations while maintaining discriminability among different instances, contrastive learning has achieved strong performance across a wide range of downstream tasks. However, the reliance on large-scale and potentially uncurated data sources introduces an important security risk: an adversary may inject a small number of poisoned samples into the unlabeled pre-training dataset and implant a hidden backdoor into the learned encoder.

In this work, we focus on preventive sample cleansing before retraining a clean contrastive encoder. Our central idea is to move from passive feature inspection to active diagnostic probing. A carefully designed probe is applied to suspicious samples, and the resulting representation changes are characterized as response features. These response features are then calibrated with a small trusted benign reference set and analyzed through local topology and density.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}
\subsection{Backdoor Attacks on Contrastive Learning}
Contrastive learning (CL) learns an encoder by enforcing consistency between
different augmented views of the same instance while separating the
representations of distinct instances. Representative frameworks instantiate
this principle in different ways. SimCLR constructs positive pairs through
stochastic data augmentation and contrasts them against other samples in a
large minibatch \cite{chen2020simclr}, whereas MoCo maintains a momentum-updated
key encoder and a queue of negative representations to provide a large and
consistent dictionary \cite{he2020moco}. After pre-training, the learned encoder
is transferred to downstream tasks through linear evaluation, fine-tuning, or
nearest-neighbor prediction. This decoupling of unlabeled pre-training from
downstream supervision enables broad reuse, but it also enlarges the attack
surface: a malicious association acquired during pre-training can persist in
the representation space and be inherited by multiple downstream models.

Backdoor attacks exploit this transferability by forcing inputs containing an
attacker-chosen trigger to produce representations associated with a target
semantic concept, while preserving normal utility on trigger-free inputs. In a
data-poisoning threat model, the adversary can inject only a small subset of
crafted samples into an otherwise benign, unlabeled corpus but does not control
the victim's training procedure. The trigger may be a visible patch or a more
subtle pattern, and the poisoned samples are constructed so that contrastive
optimization repeatedly couples the trigger with the attacker's target. At
inference time, the compromised encoder maps a triggered input toward the
target region, causing a downstream classifier to predict the target class.
Because pre-training corpora are often collected from open and weakly curated
sources, this limited data-access assumption is practically relevant.

Early work demonstrated that multimodal contrastive models can be manipulated
with a very small poisoned fraction. Carlini and Terzis showed that poisoning
image--text pairs can implant both targeted poisoning effects and patch-based
backdoors in contrastively trained models \cite{carlini2022poisoning}. In the
encoder-supply-chain setting, BadEncoder assumes control of a released
pre-trained encoder rather than its original corpus. It directly optimizes the
encoder so that triggered inputs align with reference inputs from selected
target classes, while clean representations remain close to those produced by
the original encoder \cite{jia2022badencoder}. Consequently, downstream
classifiers constructed from the compromised encoder can inherit the malicious
behavior even when their own training data and procedures are benign. These two
lines of work expose complementary risks: untrusted pre-training data can
corrupt an otherwise legitimate training pipeline, whereas an untrusted model
provider can distribute already-compromised representations.

Subsequent work has examined why straightforward poisoning is not always
reliable under contrastive objectives. Random crops, color transformations, and
other augmentations can remove or distort a trigger, while the absence of class
labels makes it difficult to bind poisoned instances consistently to a target
semantic cluster. CorruptEncoder addresses these limitations by designing
poisoned inputs and an attack objective that strengthen the association between
the trigger and target representations during CL pre-training
\cite{zhang2024corruptencoder}. Collectively, these attacks show that high clean
accuracy is not evidence of a trustworthy encoder: the malicious behavior is
conditional and may remain dormant during standard validation.

Nevertheless, existing attacks commonly depend on particular trigger families,
target reference data, augmentation robustness, or assumptions about access to
the corpus or encoder. Their effectiveness may therefore vary across CL
algorithms, poisoning budgets, and downstream tasks. More importantly, the
representation-level mechanism makes sample attribution difficult: poisoned
and benign inputs may overlap under ordinary embeddings even when they induce
different training dynamics. This limitation motivates preventive defenses
that inspect the unlabeled corpus before retraining. Rather than assuming that
a backdoor can be recognized from a fixed feature snapshot, backdoor sample
detection and cleansing should expose trigger-dependent behavior while
preserving the diversity and utility of benign pre-training data.
