\begin{abstract}
Contrastive learning has emerged as an effective paradigm for learning transferable representations from large-scale unlabeled data. However, its reliance on uncurated pre-training data exposes it to data-poisoning backdoor attacks. In this work, we study backdoored sample cleansing for unlabeled contrastive-learning datasets and develop an active diagnostic probing framework. The key idea is to transform difficult-to-separate static representations into probe-induced response representations, calibrate benign responses using a small cross-domain reference set, preserve local response topology with UMAP, and identify suspicious samples with reference-anchored DBSCAN. Extensive experiments will evaluate cleansing effectiveness, post-cleansing security and utility, and computational overhead.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Contrastive learning, self-supervised learning, backdoor attacks, poisoned sample detection, data cleansing.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ontrastive} learning (CL) has become a central paradigm for learning transferable representations from large-scale unlabeled data. Representative methods learn invariance across augmented views through instance discrimination, momentum-encoded dictionaries, or bootstrapped prediction~\cite{Chen2020SimCLR,He2020MoCo,Grill2020BYOL}. By reducing the dependence on manual annotation, CL enables pre-trained encoders to support a broad range of downstream tasks. This advantage, however, also expands the attack surface: pre-training data are often collected from large, weakly curated sources in which an adversary can inject a small number of poisoned samples. The resulting encoder may behave normally on benign inputs yet map trigger-bearing inputs toward an attacker-selected semantic region, causing downstream models to inherit a hidden backdoor~\cite{Carlini2022Poisoning,Saha2022SSLBackdoor,Liu2022PoisonedEncoder}. Furthermore, the benign and backdoor objectives are intertwined during contrastive training, yielding learning dynamics and feature distributions that differ from those of supervised backdoor attacks; consequently, defenses transferred directly from supervised learning can be ineffective~\cite{Li2023CTRL,Li2024Difficulty}.

This work considers a preventive defense at the data level: identifying and removing backdoored samples from an unlabeled pre-training set before a clean encoder is retrained. This objective is distinct from scanning a released encoder for a latent backdoor or repairing its parameters, as pursued by DECREE and SSL-Cleanse~\cite{Feng2023DECREE,Zheng2024SSLCleanse}. Existing data-oriented studies demonstrate several promising routes. PatchSearch exploits the localized influence of patch triggers, searches for trigger-like regions using a poisoned encoder, and trains a poison classifier from highly suspicious samples~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Confusion Training and ASSET actively enlarge behavioral differences between benign and poisoned samples through additional training or optimization~\cite{Qi2023Proactive,Pan2023ASSET}. AUTO instead bootstraps relatively poisonous and relatively benign subsets from the training data and iteratively refines their supervisory signals~\cite{Chen2025AUTO}. Together, these methods establish that unlabeled-data cleansing is feasible, but they also expose a tension among trigger generality, separability, optimization stability, computational cost, and the availability of trustworthy reference data.

The first limitation is insufficient separability in the original representation space. Contrastive backdoors do not necessarily produce isolated outliers: poisoned samples remain semantically meaningful, while the trigger-target association is learned jointly with benign content. Moreover, recent attacks construct the association through different mechanisms, including patch-based poisoning, frequency-domain triggers, theory-guided poisoned views, and bi-level trigger optimization~\cite{Saha2022SSLBackdoor,Li2023CTRL,Zhang2024CorruptEncoder,Sun2024BLTO}. Consequently, static embeddings, training losses, or passive clustering statistics can overlap between benign and poisoned samples, and a signature effective for one attack may not transfer to another~\cite{Pan2023ASSET,Li2024Difficulty}. Patch-specific localization provides a strong signal when a compact trigger dominates the encoder response, but this assumption narrows its applicability~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Optimization-based separation actively amplifies behavioral differences, yet its effectiveness can vary with the poisoning ratio, learning dynamics, and quality of the supervisory signal~\cite{Qi2023Proactive,Pan2023ASSET,Chen2025AUTO}. These observations motivate a diagnostic transformation that makes the latent backdoor association observable rather than requiring it to be evident in the unperturbed embedding.

The second limitation concerns the reference set. Comparing suspicious samples with trusted benign data can stabilize detection, but requiring a large clean set drawn from the same distribution as a diverse, web-scraped training corpus is often impractical; earlier attack evaluations likewise report that defenses may sacrifice utility or depend on substantial clean pre-training data~\cite{Saha2022SSLBackdoor,Liu2022PoisonedEncoder}. ASSET assumes a small clean base set, whereas AUTO shows that a distribution mismatch between reserved clean data and the training set can dominate the feature difference caused by a trigger, leading the detector to separate dataset domains instead of backdoor status~\cite{Pan2023ASSET,Chen2025AUTO}. Eliminating reference data entirely avoids this requirement, but also removes a direct benign anchor. We therefore seek a weaker and more realistic condition: a small trusted set that is semantically related to the downstream task but may originate from a different domain. The reference set should calibrate how benign samples respond to a diagnostic intervention, rather than approximate the full distribution of the unlabeled corpus.

Our key hypothesis is that a contrastive backdoor can be diagnosed through its response to a controlled probe. BLTO was originally designed to optimize a trigger that remains associated with the target throughout contrastive training~\cite{Sun2024BLTO}; here, we repurpose this attack-tailored structure as a diagnostic intervention rather than as an attack. Because poisoned representations are constrained by an existing trigger-target association, applying a BLTO-based diagnostic probe should modulate benign and poisoned samples differently. We characterize this modulation through response features that capture changes in representation magnitude, direction, similarity, and local neighborhood structure. Under this view, the detector does not ask whether an original embedding is globally unusual; it asks whether the sample's probe-induced trajectory is consistent with benign response behavior. A small cross-domain benign set can then serve as a response reference, while abnormal trajectories are expected to depart from the local topology and density of calibrated benign responses.

Based on this hypothesis, we develop an active diagnostic probing framework for backdoored sample cleansing in unlabeled CL datasets. The framework applies the BLTO diagnostic probe to the training and reference samples, extracts probe-induced response representations, and uses the trusted responses for cross-domain benign calibration. It then employs UMAP to preserve the local structure of the response manifold~\cite{McInnes2018UMAP} and a reference-anchored adaptation of DBSCAN~\cite{Ester1996DBSCAN} to identify low-density or disconnected suspicious regions. This design connects proactive separation~\cite{Qi2023Proactive,Pan2023ASSET} with representation-based anomaly detection while avoiding repeated model optimization during cleansing. Random perturbations, standard augmentations, and an unprobed baseline are included as controls to distinguish a backdoor-specific diagnostic response from generic sensitivity to input transformations.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate unlabeled backdoor sample cleansing as an active diagnostic problem and introduce a BLTO-based probe that converts weakly separable static embeddings into probe-induced response representations.
    \item We develop response features that jointly describe magnitude, direction, similarity, and neighborhood changes, providing a mechanism-oriented basis for distinguishing backdoor-constrained samples from benign samples.
    \item We introduce cross-domain benign-response calibration and combine topology-preserving UMAP with reference-anchored DBSCAN, reducing the need for a large in-distribution trusted dataset.
    \item We establish an evaluation protocol spanning multiple datasets, CL methods, backdoor attacks, and encoder architectures, with detection quality, post-cleansing security and utility, computational cost, ablations, and parameter stability as complementary criteria.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relatedwork}
\subsection{Backdoor Attacks on Contrastive Learning}
Backdoor attacks on contrastive learning exploit the fact that representations, rather than task-specific decision boundaries, are learned during unlabeled pre-training. The adversary poisons a small subset of the pre-training corpus so that the encoder preserves normal utility on benign inputs while associating a trigger with an attacker-selected semantic target. Because downstream classifiers reuse the compromised representation, the association can survive task-specific fine-tuning or linear evaluation. Early studies established that contrastive objectives are vulnerable to both targeted poisoning and persistent backdoor behavior~\cite{Carlini2022Poisoning}. SSL-Backdoor stamps a patch on images from the target category, causing a model trained without labels to transfer the trigger--target association to downstream classification~\cite{Saha2022SSLBackdoor}. PoisonedEncoder further formulates poison construction as an optimization problem and supports multiple target inputs or target classes using only poisoned pre-training samples~\cite{Liu2022PoisonedEncoder}.

Subsequent work broadens both the trigger space and the attack mechanism. CTRL embeds a trigger in the frequency domain and exploits the invariance encouraged by contrastive training, demonstrating effective attacks at low poisoning ratios~\cite{Li2023CTRL}. CorruptEncoder analyzes how random cropping and positive-pair construction affect data-poisoning attacks, then designs poisoned samples that remain effective under contrastive augmentations~\cite{Zhang2024CorruptEncoder}. BLTO jointly optimizes a trigger and a surrogate contrastive-learning process: its inner problem simulates representation learning, whereas its outer problem maintains the association between triggered samples and the target semantics~\cite{Sun2024BLTO}. These attacks differ in visibility, spatial locality, and dependence on data augmentation, so the presence of a backdoor cannot be reduced to a single patch or frequency signature.

More fundamentally, the benign and malicious tasks are coupled differently in contrastive and supervised learning. The analysis in~\cite{Li2024Difficulty} shows that contrastive representations can entangle the backdoor objective with useful semantics, making a poisoned sample neither a conventional input outlier nor an isolated feature cluster. This observation explains why a defense that relies only on static embeddings, loss values, or a supervised classifier's decision statistics may transfer poorly across contrastive algorithms and attacks. Our work therefore treats the original embedding as an insufficient diagnostic view and studies how samples respond to a controlled, attack-informed probe.

\subsection{Backdoor Defenses for Contrastive Learning}
Existing defenses can be divided into model-oriented inspection and data-oriented cleansing. Model-oriented methods determine whether a released encoder is compromised or mitigate the backdoor after training. DECREE searches for a small perturbation that makes diverse inputs share similar representations and uses the recovered perturbation to detect backdoored encoders~\cite{Feng2023DECREE}. SSL-Cleanse combines representation clustering, trigger inversion, and unlearning to detect and mitigate Trojans in self-supervised models~\cite{Zheng2024SSLCleanse}. These methods are valuable when the encoder is the only available artifact, but their primary output is a model-level diagnosis or repaired encoder rather than a sanitized version of the original pre-training corpus.

Data-oriented defenses instead identify suspicious training samples so that a new encoder can be trained on a cleaner dataset. PatchSearch trains an initial encoder on the contaminated corpus, localizes salient candidate patches, evaluates their ability to induce anomalous behavior when pasted onto other images, and iteratively trains a poison classifier~\cite{Tejankar2023PatchSearch}. Its localization signal is well matched to compact patch triggers but is less directly applicable to distributed, frequency-domain, or sample-specific patterns. Confusion Training actively introduces additional label noise to decouple benign and backdoor correlations and thereby expose poisoned samples~\cite{Qi2023Proactive}. Although developed in a supervised setting, it motivates the broader principle that a diagnostic intervention can reveal a latent backdoor more clearly than passive observation.

ASSET extends active separation across supervised, self-supervised, and transfer-learning paradigms by optimizing an offset between the untrusted data and a small clean base set~\cite{Pan2023ASSET}. Its trusted anchor improves identifiability, but distribution mismatch between the base and training sets can become a competing separation signal. AUTO removes the external clean-data requirement by mining relatively benign and relatively poisonous subsets from the contaminated corpus and bootstrapping their refinement~\cite{Chen2025AUTO}. This reduces reliance on a curated reference set, while making the result dependent on the quality of the initially mined supervision and repeated optimization. In contrast, our framework uses a small semantically related but potentially cross-domain reference set only to calibrate benign \emph{responses}; it seeks separation through a single diagnostic transformation rather than requiring the reference set to reproduce the full training distribution.

\subsection{Representation-Based Anomaly Detection}
Representation-space analysis is a major line of backdoor sample detection. Spectral Signatures centers deep features and removes examples with large projections onto a dominant singular direction associated with poisoning~\cite{Tran2018Spectral}. SPECTRE strengthens this idea with robust covariance estimation, which amplifies a weak spectral signal when the poisoning ratio is too small for ordinary covariance statistics~\cite{Hayase2021SPECTRE}. Beatrix instead summarizes high-order activation correlations with Gram matrices and learns class-conditional benign statistics, enabling detection of triggers that vary across inputs~\cite{Ma2023Beatrix}. These methods demonstrate that a successful backdoor leaves measurable structure in learned representations, but their supervised formulations commonly exploit labels, predicted classes, or sufficiently coherent within-class feature statistics.

Those conditions are not directly available in an unlabeled contrastive-learning corpus. Global statistics may be dominated by semantic diversity, while class-conditional partitioning based on a compromised encoder can reinforce the very association being detected. Moreover, passive features can overlap when the backdoor and benign objectives are tightly coupled~\cite{Li2024Difficulty}. Our approach changes the object being analyzed: instead of treating the original embedding as the anomaly signal, it constructs a response representation from the change induced by a controlled probe. UMAP is then used to preserve local neighborhood structure in this response space~\cite{McInnes2018UMAP}, and a reference-anchored variant of DBSCAN identifies low-density or disconnected samples without specifying the number of clusters~\cite{Ester1996DBSCAN}. Thus, UMAP and DBSCAN serve as geometry-preserving and density-estimation components; the defense signal itself is the probe-induced response calibrated by trusted benign references.

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:threatmodel}
\subsection{Attacker Capabilities}
Let $\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{b}\cup\mathcal{D}_{p}$ denote the unlabeled pre-training corpus, where $\mathcal{D}_{b}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{p}$ are the benign and poisoned subsets, respectively. The poisoning ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \rho=\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{p}|}{|\mathcal{D}|}.
    \label{eq:poisoningratio}
\end{equation}
The defender does not know $\rho$; we consider the conventional data-poisoning regime in which poisoned samples are a minority. The attacker selects a target semantic class $y_t$, a trigger parameter $\tau$, and a trigger transformation $\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\cdot)$. The transformation may be a visible patch, a distributed or frequency-domain pattern, or an optimized sample-dependent perturbation. Poisoned examples may contain target-class content or be constructed through an attack-specific optimization, and they participate in pre-training under the same augmentations as benign data.

The attack aims to obtain an encoder $f_{\theta}$ that retains benign representation quality while causing a downstream predictor $g_{\phi}\circ f_{\theta}$ to map trigger-bearing inputs to $y_t$. The attacker may know the contrastive algorithm, encoder architecture, augmentation family, training hyperparameters, and the design of the cleansing method, and may optimize poisons using a surrogate training process. The attacker can inject or replace training samples but cannot modify the defender's cleansing code, post-cleansing retraining procedure, or trusted reference set. In the adaptive setting, the attacker knows the distribution from which diagnostic probes are constructed but does not observe the realized probe parameters or random seed before committing the poisoned corpus.

\subsection{Defender Capabilities}
The defender possesses the complete unlabeled corpus $\mathcal{D}$ and a potentially compromised encoder trained on it. The defender can run forward inference, access intermediate or final representations, apply input transformations, and retrain an encoder after suspicious samples have been removed. No ground-truth class labels, poison annotations, trigger pattern, target class, attack identity, or poisoning ratio are available for $\mathcal{D}$. The defense must therefore operate without fixing the number of poisons in advance.

In addition, the defender has a small trusted benign reference set $\mathcal{R}$. Its samples are semantically related to the intended downstream domain but may come from a different acquisition process or dataset; hence, $\mathcal{R}$ is not assumed to match the full distribution of $\mathcal{D}_{b}$. Semantic labels may be used to curate the coverage of $\mathcal{R}$, but they are not transferred to $\mathcal{D}$ and are not used as poison supervision. The defender can apply the same diagnostic probe to $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{R}$ and use the resulting trusted responses to calibrate benign local geometry.

\subsection{Assumptions and Defense Objective}
We make three explicit assumptions. First, $\mathcal{R}$ is benign and cannot be modified by the attacker; it may be cross-domain but must retain semantic relevance to the application. Second, poisoned samples remain a minority, although their exact prevalence is unknown. Third, probe construction contains run-specific randomness that is hidden until cleansing begins. This last assumption does not conceal the defense algorithm: an adaptive attacker may optimize against the probe family, but cannot tailor each poisoned sample to the exact probe realization. The randomized-probe assumption will be tested by repeating the defense across independent seeds and, where feasible, by evaluating adaptive attacks against the known probe distribution.

Let $\delta(x_i)\in\{0,1\}$ be the cleansing decision for $x_i\in\mathcal{D}$, where $1$ denotes a suspicious sample. The estimated poisoned set and the retained corpus are
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{p}=\{x_i\in\mathcal{D}:\delta(x_i)=1\},\qquad
    \widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{b}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{p}.
    \label{eq:cleansingsets}
\end{equation}
The immediate objective is to recover as much of $\mathcal{D}_{p}$ as possible while minimizing removal of benign samples. The end-to-end objective is that retraining on $\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{b}$ substantially reduces attack success on triggered inputs while preserving clean downstream utility. Accordingly, detection precision, recall, and false-positive rate must be reported together with post-cleansing attack success rate and clean accuracy; no single clustering score is treated as sufficient evidence of a successful defense.
